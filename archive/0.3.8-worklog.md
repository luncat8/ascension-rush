# Worklog — 0.3.8 System chart: other bodies through the wrap/log projection

## Starting point

0.3.7 shipped an inter-planet **sky dome**: `sky.projectBody` put a star, two
planets and two moons into an azimuthal-equidistant band across the top of the
screen, as round sprites with a clamped radius. The ship, the pads and the
ground lived in a different map — wrap-X / log-Y — and the two never mixed.

That is what this increment had to remove. The request was specific: other
bodies must go through *the same* wrapped-circle-to-line, log-scale projection
as the ground, and when the ship moves to another body the chart must re-seat
on it smoothly, so that body's surface becomes the flat ground line with no
snap.

All 16 suites were green at the start.

## The two wrong turns, recorded because they were expensive

**Wrong turn 1 — vertical log only.** The first attempt kept the existing map
(independent horizontal px/metre, vertical `log1p(altitude/knee)`) and drew each
body as the lens its sphere casts through it. The lens maths was right and the
map was identical to the deck's, so the deck did not move. But a distant body's
true silhouette through *that* map is a two-pixel sliver, and stretching it to
readability made every body look like a flattened smudge. The reason is
structural: the two axes carried different pixels per e-fold of radius, so a
round sphere could never come out round.

**Wrong turn 2 — fit the system anyway.** Making the ceiling reach the star
(8.9 million km) put every body on screen, but compressed the atmosphere and the
whole 10 m – 10 km gameplay band into the top third. The deck stopped being a
gameplay map.

## The decisive reference

The user pointed at `github.com/luncat8/2d-star-system-wrap`, which is the same
projection. Reading `log-projection.js` and `log-render.js` settled it in one
step:

```
x = ox + s · atan2(dx, dy)
y = oy − s · ln(distance)
```

**One scale `s` for both axes.** That is the whole difference. A followed body
sets `oy = line + s·ln(R)`, so its surface lands on the screen line and its
centre becomes the polar singularity below it; its interior then maps down the
screen as a cutaway. Bodies are drawn by sampling their own sphere — for each
angle the ray hits it twice, giving the near and far limbs — so the shape is
genuinely the silhouette.

The reduction that made it fit this game: with the pivot on the active world,

```
y = groundY − s · ln(1 + wy / R)      x = ox + s · wx / R
```

which is exactly the existing wrap-X / log-altitude chart with the world's
radius as its log knee. So there is **one** projection, and the deck view is
its closed form — not a second projection bolted on.

## Implemented

- **`js/coords.js`** — the log-polar map in both directions, with
  `camera.flat` selecting the closed-form rectilinear spelling for the deck.
  The world point is placed at its own radius from the world centre and then
  measured against the pivot; the inverse adds the pivot back, so the round trip
  is exact (measured 7e-12 m on the deck, and exact while seated elsewhere).
- **`js/camera.js`** — owns the log-polar pose (`pivotX/Y`, `pivotRadius`,
  `worldRadius`, `ox`, `oy`, `scalePx`) alongside the range fields the rest of
  the game already reads. `setPolar` derives the shared scale from the farthest
  charted extent.
- **`js/sky.js`** — the catalog, the damped shared-scale fit, `seatPivot` /
  `reset`, and `outline()` which solves both limbs of every body's sphere per
  sample angle, in screen space, per frame. No allocations in the hot path: two
  `Float64Array`s sized once from `constants.sky.outlineSamples`.
- **`js/render.js`** — `lensPath` draws that polygon; `drawSkyBody` fills it,
  shades the side turned from the star and labels it; `drawGroundBody` draws
  the seated body's interior below the ground line; `render.drawChart` is the
  sky-and-ground half of `render.draw`, reusable by the test page.
- **`js/constants.js`** — the dome constants (`skyBandFraction`,
  `skyHorizonAngle`, `minBodyRadius`, `maxBodyRadius`, `approachLogSpan`,
  `bodyScaleFactor`) are gone; `chartTopFraction`, `fitSampleSeconds`,
  `fitGrowTau`, `fitCloseTau`, `outlineSamples` and `pivotSeconds` replace them.

## The hand-over

The chart is seated on exactly one body, its pivot, whose surface is the ground
line. Seating it on another is continuous because the pose is **solved**, not
interpolated:

1. Capture the destination body's surface point nearest the ship — the anchor —
   and where it currently sits on screen.
2. Ease the pole to the new body's centre and ease the shared scale.
3. Every frame, solve `ox` and `oy` from the anchor against the live pole.

Because the anchor's angle and distance are both read against the *current*
pole, its screen position does not change while the pole slides. The chart
travels underneath it, and the new surface arrives as the ground line.

Measured, `node` drives the same update the frame loop does:

```
world -> Lune   anchor drift 0.0 px   (87 frames)
Lune  -> world  anchor drift 0.0 px   (87 frames)
Lune  -> Pale   anchor drift 0.0 px   (87 frames)
```

The **ship is not the anchor** and does move; it is a different point of the
plane. An earlier version anchored on the ship and produced 700 px jumps,
because it was trying to hold still the one thing that legitimately travels.

Two bugs found by measurement rather than by eye:

- The inverse forgot to add the pivot back, so a world point projected through
  the chart did not come back while seated elsewhere. `sky-test` caught it as a
  4,700 m error on a 1,234 m point.
- Clearing `pivot.reseating` *before* `applyPose` made the last frame of every
  hand-over jump to the settled offsets. The flag now clears after the pose is
  drawn.

## Known limits

- A hand-over that jumps the pivot across most of the system can leave the view
  mostly empty mid-morph, because the anchor is held and everything else sweeps
  past. The hop page shows it clearly. It is continuous, but it is a fast sweep
  rather than a prettied transition.
- The shaded half of a lens uses a star-angle approximation. It reads correctly
  on the deck and on nearby bodies; it is not a lighting model.
- The chart's `ceiling` is still the world's own range while seated on the
  world, so a body whose lens would be above it is simply off-screen until the
  ship climbs. That is deliberate: the deck must stay a gameplay map.
- No physics knows about the pivot. Gravity, drag, collisions and the coast
  predictor remain the active world's single inverse-square field.
